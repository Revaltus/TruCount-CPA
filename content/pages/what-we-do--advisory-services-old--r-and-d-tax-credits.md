## What Are R&D Tax Credits?

The federal Research and Development (R&D) tax credit rewards businesses for the work they're already doing: testing a new seed treatment, refining an irrigation system, developing custom software, or reworking a manufacturing process. Congress created the credit under IRC Section 41 to keep innovation happening on American soil, and South Dakota businesses can often stack a state credit right alongside it.

Here's what sets the R&D credit apart from a standard deduction. A deduction lowers your taxable income. This credit lowers your tax bill dollar for dollar. If your business spent $200,000 on qualifying wages, supplies, and contract research last year, the credit reduces what you owe the IRS directly, not just the number your tax rate gets applied to. For farmers experimenting with new hybrids or agribusinesses building custom equipment, that difference adds up fast.

## Does Your Business Qualify?

Most business owners assume the R&D credit belongs to Silicon Valley. It doesn't. The IRS applies a four-part test, and if your work meets it, the label on your invoices doesn't matter.

- Permitted purpose: The activity aims to develop or improve a product, process, formula, or software, whether that's a new grain bin design or a proprietary billing system.
- Elimination of uncertainty: You didn't know upfront whether the approach would work, how to design it, or which method to use.
- Process of experimentation: You tested, modeled, or evaluated alternatives, think field trials, prototype builds, or simulation runs.
- Technological in nature: The work relies on principles of engineering, biology, chemistry, computer science, or a similar hard science.

Qualifying costs typically include employee wages for time spent on research, supplies consumed during testing, and payments to contract researchers such as agronomists or engineers. A crop input trial, a new fabrication technique, or a custom accounting integration can all meet the bar.

## How the R&D Credit Calculation Works

There are two ways to calculate the R&D credit, and picking the right one changes your result. The regular research credit compares your current qualified research expenses (QREs) against a base amount tied to your company's historical research spending and gross receipts, then applies a 20% rate to the excess.

The Alternative Simplified Credit (ASC) method suits smaller and mid-sized businesses better because it doesn't require decades of historical data. It looks at your average QREs from the prior three years, then applies a 14% rate to the amount your current spending exceeds half that average. If you had no qualifying research in the prior three years, a flat 6% rate applies instead.

Neither method wins automatically. The right choice depends on your spending history, your gross receipts, and how well your records support each calculation. **FAQ Block:**

**Q: Can a small farm operation actually claim the R&D tax credit?**
A: Yes. Size doesn't disqualify you. If you tested new seed varieties, soil amendments, or equipment modifications and weren't certain the approach would work upfront, that activity can meet the IRS four-part test regardless of your farm's revenue.

**Q: What records do I need to support an R&D credit claim?**
A: You need contemporaneous documentation: payroll records tied to research time, project notes describing what you tested and why, supply invoices, and contractor agreements. TruCount CPA PC helps set up this tracking before filing season, not after.

**Q: Is the R&D credit available at the state level in South Dakota?**
A: South Dakota doesn't currently offer a standalone state R&D credit, but businesses operating in Western Minnesota or Ohio may have state-level credits to stack with the federal credit. We review your entity's footprint to check every option.

**Q: How far back can I claim missed R&D credits?**
A: Businesses can generally amend the last three tax years to capture missed R&D credits, and unused credits can carry forward up to 20 years. We review prior filings during your assessment to see what's still on the table.
