Buying a cabin at Lake Poinsett or a place in the Black Hills sounds like a lifestyle decision. For the IRS, it's a math problem. The number of days you personally use that property versus the number of days you rent it out determines whether your second home works like a tax shelter, a rental business, or something in between. Get the days wrong and you could lose deductions you assumed were automatic, or trigger income reporting you didn't expect.

Here's how that classification works and what it means for your return.

## Three tax categories, one house

The IRS doesn't ask whether you call it a vacation home, a cabin, or a rental. It looks at two numbers: personal-use days and rental days. Those numbers sort your property into one of three buckets under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

| Situation | Personal use | Rental use | How it's taxed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal residence with minimal rental | Any amount | 14 days or fewer | Rental income isn't reported at all; no rental deductions |
| Dwelling used as a home | More than 14 days, or more than 10% of rental days | Any amount | Rental income reported; expenses limited to rental income, no loss allowed |
| Rental property | 14 days or fewer, or 10% or less of rental days | Any amount | Rental income reported; losses may be deductible, subject to passive activity rules |

That middle category catches more owners than you'd expect, especially with lake properties the family uses heavily in July and August and rents out sparingly the rest of the year.

## The 14-day rule that lets you skip reporting entirely

If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, you don't report the rental income at all, no matter how much you charged. Rent a lake cabin for one busy weekend and collect $1,800? That income never touches your Form 1040. This is sometimes called the Augusta rule.

The tradeoff: you can't deduct any rental-related expenses, including depreciation, since the IRS treats the home as fully personal for the year. It works in your favor only when the income is the goal, not the deductions.

## Second home vs investment property, and why the label matters

The IRS treats these two very differently, and the distinction comes down to that personal-use threshold again.

If you spend more than 14 days a year in the home, or more than 10% of the days it's rented, it's a dwelling used as a home under Section 280A. Mortgage interest can still be deductible on Schedule A, subject to the $750,000 combined mortgage debt limit. But rental deductions are capped at your rental income, so you can zero it out but never create a loss.

Keep personal use to 14 days or fewer (or 10% of rental days, whichever is greater), and it becomes a rental property. Now you're on Schedule E and losses become possible, subject to passive activity rules and a $25,000 special allowance for active participants that phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of adjusted gross income.

## A concrete example

Say you buy a cabin near Oakwood Lakes for $240,000. You use it 20 days and rent it 40 days for $9,600. Because 20 personal days is more than 14 and more than 10% of 40 rental days, it's a dwelling used as a home. You report the $9,600 and deduct prorated expenses only up to that amount. No loss, even if your expenses ran to $12,000.

Now flip it. You use the cabin 10 days and rent it 55 days for $13,200. Ten personal days is below both thresholds, so it's a rental property. Depreciation applies, expenses can exceed income, and if you actively manage the bookings and maintenance, you may deduct up to $25,000 of the loss against other income, depending on your AGI.

Ten days of personal use is the difference between a capped deduction and real tax-reducing losses, and it's a number most buyers don't track until it's too late to fix.

## What you can actually deduct once it's a rental

Once personal use falls under the threshold and the property qualifies as a rental, deductible expenses typically include:

- Mortgage interest and property taxes, prorated for rental days
- Homeowners insurance, prorated
- Utilities and HOA dues, prorated
- Repairs and routine maintenance
- Property management or cleaning fees tied to rental turnover
- Depreciation on the rental-use portion of the property

Expenses are split by day count, so your calendar matters as much as your receipts. [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) walks through how the allocation works when a property serves both purposes in the same year.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?** If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the tax year, you don't have to report the rental income at all, regardless of the amount collected. In exchange, you can't deduct any rental-related expenses for that year, since the IRS treats the property as entirely personal use.

**Can I deduct a loss on a second home I rent out?** Only if the property qualifies as a rental under IRS rules, meaning your personal use is 14 days or fewer, or no more than 10% of the days it's rented, whichever is greater. If personal use exceeds that threshold, your rental deductions are capped at your rental income and no loss is allowed.

**Is a second home the same as an investment property for tax purposes?** No. A second home you use personally more than 14 days a year (or more than 10% of rental days) is treated as a dwelling used as a home, with limited rental deductions. An investment property with minimal personal use follows different rules, including depreciation and potential loss deductions under passive activity limits.

**Do I need to report rental income if I only rent my cabin out occasionally?** If total rental days for the year are 14 or fewer, no, that income is exempt from reporting under the 14-day rule. Once you cross 15 rental days, all rental income becomes reportable, and your deductions depend on how many days you personally used the property that same year.
