*By TruCount CPA  PC, Accounting, tax and advisory firm in South Dakota, serving clients in the Brookings area and throughout the Midwest*

Most of the farmers and business owners we sit down with in Brookings built their wealth the hard way, and they built it lopsided on purpose. A farm operation might have seventy or eighty percent of net worth locked into land and equipment. Professional service owners often have theirs tied up in the practice. That concentration isn't a mistake, it's how equity gets built. The mistake shows up later, when the same owner treats an outside brokerage account the way they treat a bin of stored grain: fill it once, check on it occasionally, and assume it's fine. It usually isn't.

## Risk drifts even when you don't touch a thing

A portfolio that started at 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds doesn't stay there on its own. After a strong run in equities, that same account can quietly become 75/25 within three or four years, carrying more risk than the owner ever agreed to. Federal Reserve research points to this pattern: households that don't actively manage allocation tend to accumulate concentration risk without realizing it ([Federal Reserve Notes on Household Finance and Investment Behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm)). Rebalancing is what corrects that drift. Skipping it isn't neutral. It's a decision, just one nobody signed off on.

## What balance looks like when land is already half your net worth

Standard allocation advice assumes your investment account is your main risk. For many farm families, it isn't. If half your net worth is in farmland and a combine that depreciates every season, your investment portfolio needs to diversify against that concentration, not repeat it. Loading up on farmland REITs or commodity-heavy funds inside your IRA doubles down on the same weather and commodity-price risk you already carry on the balance sheet. A genuinely balanced portfolio for a row-crop operator or an agribusiness owner often leans toward assets that move independently of grain prices and land values: broad equity index exposure, fixed income, and enough liquidity to cover a bad year without selling ground.

## Rebalancing is a tax decision as much as an investment one

Selling appreciated stock to bring your allocation back in line triggers a capital gain, and the timing of that gain matters more for farm and business income than most portfolios ever get credit for. Farm income swings hard year to year, which is why Schedule J income averaging exists: a strong crop year plus realized gains from a rebalance can push a farmer into a higher bracket, and averaging that income back over the prior three years can soften the hit. Deferred grain contracts work the same lever from a different angle, letting a farmer choose which tax year a grain sale lands in. Once you're managing income timing with a grain contract, timing a rebalance around the same tax year is the same planning conversation. Picture a landowner who sells appreciated stock in a year when an excess fertility deduction on purchased ground is also available: the deduction can offset part of the gain. None of this happens by accident, it happens when someone looks at the whole picture before December. ## Concentrated equity in the business you run is its own imbalance

If you own a professional service business, your largest asset probably doesn't trade on any exchange. Every dollar of equity sits in one illiquid basket: your firm. A balanced approach means building outside assets deliberately and planning how a future sale gets taxed, since that's the biggest rebalancing event most owners will face. ## A rebalancing habit beats a rebalancing event

Risk tolerance isn't fixed. A 38-year-old farmer two years into taking over the operation can absorb more volatility than that same farmer at 61, weighing a transition to the next generation. An annual rebalancing check, tied to your actual tax picture, catches both the drift in your allocation and the shift in what you can actually afford to risk. The AICPA's Personal Financial Planning resources make the same case for a coordinated, ongoing review rather than a one-time allocation decision ([AICPA Personal Financial Planning Resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning)). ## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**

Once a year is a reasonable baseline, tied to a specific month. Pairing that review with tax planning season lets you decide the rebalance and its tax bill together.

**Does rebalancing always trigger a tax bill?**

Only in a taxable account, and only on gains you realize when you sell. Rebalancing inside an IRA or 401(k) doesn't trigger a current tax event.

**How does farm income averaging affect a portfolio rebalance?**

Schedule J lets a farmer spread a high-income year, crop sale gains included, back over the prior three years' tax rates. If a rebalance lands in a year with strong farm income, coordinating the two can meaningfully lower the combined tax bill compared to treating them as separate decisions.

**What does a balanced portfolio look like for someone whose net worth is mostly land or business equity?**

It looks lighter on assets correlated to your existing concentration, land, commodities, or your own industry, and heavier on broad, liquid diversification you can access without selling the farm or the practice. The right mix depends on your actual balance sheet, not a generic age-based rule.
