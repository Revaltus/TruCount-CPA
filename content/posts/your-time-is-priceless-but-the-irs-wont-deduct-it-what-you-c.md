*By TruCount CPA  PC, Accounting, tax and advisory firm in South Dakota, serving clients in the Brookings area and throughout the Midwest*

Every fall, someone at a nonprofit board meeting or a 4-H fundraiser asks the same question: can I deduct the time I spent doing this? The honest answer disappoints most people. The IRS has never allowed a deduction for the value of your labor. But many volunteers assume that means nothing about their volunteering is deductible, and that can leave real money on the table every April.

The out-of-pocket costs tied to volunteer work are often deductible. Here's what counts, what doesn't, and how to document it so the deduction holds up.

## Why your time doesn't show up on Schedule A

Your hours are real and valuable, but they have no dollar figure attached for tax purposes. An accountant who spends 20 hours on a nonprofit's books for free can't deduct what those hours would have billed. A contractor who frames a house on a Saturday can't deduct the going rate for framing labor.

What changes the math is money you actually spend out of your own pocket. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) draws a clear line between the value of services (never deductible) and unreimbursed expenses connected to those services (deductible, with conditions).

## The organization has to qualify before anything else does

The organization you're volunteering for has to be a qualified charity, generally a 501(c)(3) or similar tax-exempt organization. A political campaign, a for-profit business, or an informal group without exempt status won't produce a deduction, however good the cause. Churches, most 4-H and FFA chapters, hospitals, and registered nonprofits typically qualify. Not sure? The [IRS Charities & Nonprofits resource hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) lets you check exempt status before you start tracking expenses.

This matters in a farming community like Brookings, where giving often runs through grain donations, 4-H livestock show sponsorships, or unpaid co-op board service. Some of those qualify and some don't, so confirm up front.

## Mileage, supplies, and travel volunteers actually miss

Three categories cover most of the deductible expenses volunteers overlook:

**Mileage.** If you drive your own vehicle for volunteer work, you can deduct actual gas and oil costs, or use the standard charitable mileage rate, set by statute at 14 cents per mile. It's modest, but it adds up if you deliver Meals on Wheels three times a week or haul supplies to a fairgrounds cleanup.

**Supplies and uniforms.** A uniform the charity requires that isn't suitable for everyday wear is deductible. So are supplies you buy and donate, like paint for a community mural or feed for a 4-H demonstration barn, as long as you weren't reimbursed.

**Travel away from home.** Reasonable travel costs, including lodging and meals, can be deductible for a convention, disaster relief effort, or mission trip with no significant personal vacation element. If the trip has meaningful recreation attached, the IRS can disallow it, so review this one with a CPA first.

What never qualifies: the value of your time, lost wages from time off work, and regular clothing you happen to wear while volunteering.

## Keep the paper trail or lose the deduction

For any single contribution of $250 or more, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization describing your services and confirming you received nothing of value in return. Below that, your own mileage logs, receipts, and bank or credit card statements need to be good enough to reconstruct the expense.

Most people who lose this deduction on audit had a real expense. They just can't rebuild six months of mileage from memory in March. A simple habit works best: log the date, destination, purpose, and miles right when you drive.

## Itemizing is the real gatekeeper

None of this matters unless you itemize on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction. Many volunteers with well-documented expenses still take the standard deduction because it's larger. The records aren't wasted, though. Charitable expenses stack with mortgage interest, state taxes, and other itemized items, and in a year with a larger gift or a big medical expense, itemizing can come out ahead. Reviewing it each year is where a year-round conversation with your accountant pays off.

## For board members and business owners who volunteer through the business

Professional service owners and farm operators often serve on co-op boards, ag lender advisory committees, or industry nonprofit boards. When that work overlaps with the business, the rules get more nuanced. A farmer who donates grain directly to a qualified charity may get better treatment than one who sells the grain and donates the cash, because the donated commodity can avoid triggering income. A business owner who underwrites a 4-H show as a sponsorship may have a deductible marketing expense instead of a charitable gift, with different substantiation rules.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of professional services I donate to a nonprofit, like legal or accounting work?**

No. The IRS disallows a deduction for the value of services, no matter the skill or your usual rate. Only unreimbursed out-of-pocket expenses, like mileage or supplies, are deductible.

**Do I need a receipt for every small volunteer expense?**

For $250 or more, you need written acknowledgment from the organization. Below that, you still need reliable records, such as a mileage log, receipts, or bank statements. Track it when it happens.

**What's the mileage rate for driving to volunteer activities?**

The standard charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile, set by statute rather than adjusted for inflation like the business rate. You can deduct actual gas and oil costs instead. Either way, keep a log of dates and destinations.

**Is it worth tracking volunteer expenses if I usually take the standard deduction?**

Often yes. A larger gift, a medical expense, or an income change can make itemizing worthwhile. Keeping records costs almost nothing and preserves the option.
