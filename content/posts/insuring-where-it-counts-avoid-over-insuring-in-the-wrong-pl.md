Ask five people how much insurance coverage you need and you'll get five different rules of thumb: ten times your salary in life insurance, full replacement cost on every building, a flat $1 million in liability because that's what everyone else carries. None of those numbers come from anything specific to your farm, your service business, or your family's actual finances. They're guesses dressed up as guidance. The better question isn't which policies to buy. It's how much financial exposure you actually have, and how much of it you can afford to absorb yourself before insurance needs to cover the rest.

This conversation comes up at TruCount CPA PC more often through tax planning than through insurance shopping. Premiums jump, the equipment list grows, or a second employee joins, and coverage bought years ago no longer fits. Some parts end up underinsured, while others sit overinsured, draining cash every month for protection nobody needs. Both mistakes cost real money for farmers and service business owners across Brookings, SD.

## Insurance is a math problem, not a shopping list

Every insurance decision comes down to one calculation: what does it cost if this specific bad thing happens, and what does it cost each year to transfer that risk to an insurer? A grain bin fire, a slip-and-fall lawsuit, an injured combine operator, a key employee who quits mid-season. Each has a real dollar figure, even if it's uncomfortable to estimate.

Once you know that number, [the coverage decision gets simpler](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl-2). If losing a $40,000 piece of equipment wouldn't change your balance sheet, a higher deductible may make more sense than a premium. If losing your only combine during harvest would stop cash flow for months, that deserves real coverage, not the cheapest quote you found online.

## Start with what you can't afford to lose

Net worth is the anchor. Picture a farmer with 800 acres, $3.2 million in land and equipment, and $180,000 in operating debt. Their exposure centers on land, equipment, and hired labor. Now picture a service business owner with $150,000 in revenue and one employee. Their exposure is professional liability and the cash flow gap if that employee is out for eight weeks.

An umbrella policy should extend coverage past where your homeowners or auto liability limits stop, not duplicate them. Many people buy one without checking that it starts where their underlying policies end, and pay for a gap they never closed.

## Where over-insuring quietly drains cash flow

Over-insuring rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a $500 deductible where a $2,500 deductible would cut the premium by 15% and cash reserves could absorb the difference. It looks like life insurance sized for thirty years of income when the mortgage is paid off in twelve. It looks like full coverage on a truck worth less than the annual premium.

Here's a simple comparison:

| Coverage type | Common default | Right-sized based on exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Auto/equipment deductible | $500 | $2,500, backed by 3 months of cash reserve |
| Life insurance | 10x salary, flat | Income replacement gap plus outstanding debt, minus liquid assets |
| Business liability | $1 million, standard | Matched to largest single asset or contract exposure |
| Personal umbrella | Skipped or under-limits | Starts where auto/home liability ends, covers net worth above $500,000 |

Revisit deductibles every year. Raising them by $1,000 per item across a farm's policies can free up several hundred dollars in premium, money better spent building the cash reserve that makes a higher deductible safe. The same cash-flow thinking applies to leasing or buying a vehicle, as in [our breakdown of leasing versus buying a business vehicle](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense).

## A risk-based approach for agribusiness and service businesses

Farmers and agribusiness operators carry specific exposures: crop and hail coverage sized to planted acres and current commodity prices, equipment coverage that keeps pace with replacement costs, and liability coverage for hired labor during planting and harvest.

Service businesses have a different profile. Professional liability, sometimes called errors and omissions coverage, protects against claims tied to advice or work product. A small consulting practice needs less property coverage than a farm, but may need more liability protection relative to its size.

Both groups tend to overlook business interruption coverage, which replaces lost income if a fire or equipment failure stops operations for weeks. Close that gap before it becomes a crisis.

## Running the numbers before you buy

An insurance needs analysis starts with three numbers: liquid net worth, fixed net worth, and annual cash flow after debt service. From there, you can see how much of a loss you could absorb without borrowing or selling assets, and how much to transfer to an insurer.

Insurance decisions also intersect with tax planning. Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, as outlined in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), so the after-tax cost of a policy is often lower than the sticker price.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do I actually need?**

There's no universal percentage. Start by calculating your liquid net worth, your fixed assets at risk, and your annual cash flow after debt payments. Coverage should fill the gap between what a major loss would cost and what you could absorb without borrowing or selling assets.

**Do I need business interruption insurance for a farm or service business?**

If a fire, equipment failure, or extended illness would stop your income for more than a few weeks, business interruption coverage is worth pricing out. It replaces lost income during the shutdown, which standard property coverage doesn't do.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**

Generally yes. Business insurance premiums are typically deductible as an ordinary and necessary business expense under IRS guidelines outlined in [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). The specifics depend on the policy type and how the business is structured.

**How often should I review my coverage?**

At least once a year, and any time your asset base, revenue, or employee count changes meaningfully. A combine purchase, a new hire, or a jump in land value are all good triggers for a fresh review rather than waiting for renewal season.
