*By TruCount CPA  PC, Accounting, tax and advisory firm in South Dakota, serving clients in the Brookings area and throughout the Midwest*

Say a client's golden retriever "guards the office" by napping under the desk. Is he a business expense? Almost always no. But a handful of narrow, well-documented situations exist where a dog really can reduce what you owe. If you're a farmer, a service-business owner, or someone managing a chronic condition, it's worth [knowing exactly where that line sits](/resources/unless-its-working-for-you-your-dog-isnt-a-tax-deduction-2), so you claim what you're entitled to and skip the deduction that gets flagged in an audit.

This matters more in Brookings, SD than in most places. Farmers and agribusiness operators here often keep working dogs for livestock protection, and service-based business owners sometimes keep a dog on site for security. The IRS doesn't care about breed or how much you love the animal. It cares how the dog functions in your business or medical care, and whether you can prove it.

## Why pets don't qualify as a deduction

[IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) sets the baseline rule for business expenses: a cost has to be ordinary and necessary for carrying on your trade or business. A family pet, even one that lives at a business address, fails that test because the primary reason it exists is personal companionship, not commerce. Vet bills, food, grooming, and boarding for a household dog are personal expenses, just like your own groceries.

The confusion starts because a dog does *something* useful, like barking at the mail carrier or making clients smile in the lobby. Usefulness isn't the standard. The dog's role has to be documented, ongoing, and integral to how the business operates.

## Service and guide dogs get real tax relief

Most people miss this one because they're looking in the wrong publication. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows the cost of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal as a medical expense if the dog assists someone with a visual, hearing, or other physical disability. That includes food, grooming, and veterinary care.

The catch is the same one that applies to every medical deduction: your total qualifying medical expenses have to clear 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and you have to itemize. That's a big reason [tax-advantaged planning matters](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi) when medical costs are in the picture. A service dog alone may not get you there, but if you're already itemizing, those expenses belong in the math. This is a service animal tax deduction, not a therapy dog deduction, and the distinction is one many people miss by [waiting until year-end to plan](/resources/the-biggest-tax-mistake-wealthy-individuals-make-waiting-unt).

## Therapy dogs and the fine print the IRS cares about

A therapy dog that visits nursing homes or schools for emotional comfort is not automatically deductible, even when the visits are valuable. The IRS treats emotional support animals differently from trained service animals performing a specific task tied to a diagnosed disability. A therapy dog deduction under IRS rules generally requires task-specific training related to a medical condition, documented usually by a treating physician.

If your dog's role is emotional support without that training, don't count on the medical expense deduction. The dog may be worth every penny to your wellbeing, but the tax code hasn't caught up to that kind of value.

## Guard dogs can be a legitimate business expense

A guard dog business expense is one of the more defensible dog-related deductions for owners who store equipment, inventory, or livestock feed on-site. It has to look like a real security decision, not a rationalization for keeping a pet at the shop.

That means the dog is a breed suited to guarding, it's kept primarily at the business rather than the owner's home, and there's a documented reason, like a history of theft, high-value equipment, or an insurance requirement. Food, veterinary care, and training can then be deducted as a business dog tax write-off. Keep receipts and a simple log or photo record showing the dog living and working at the business.

## Working dogs on the farm get different treatment

Agricultural operations get some of the clearest footing here. Livestock guardian dogs, herding dogs, and dogs used for predator control around grain storage all have a defensible business purpose under the same ordinary-and-necessary standard in Publication 535.

The expenses that typically qualify include:

| Expense type | Likely deductible if | Not deductible if |
|---|---|---|
| Food and vet care | Dog lives and works primarily on the farm | Dog is primarily a household pet |
| Training costs | Training is specific to herding, guarding, or predator control | General obedience training for a pet |
| Purchase price | Bought specifically for working purposes, with breed and use documented | Purchased as a family dog that happens to live on the property |

If you run a breeding operation as an actual business, with sales, a business bank account, and consistent records, the dogs become business assets. Feed, veterinary costs, stud fees, and depreciation on breeding stock can be legitimate deductions. The IRS scrutinizes hobby versus business closely, so talk it through before assuming side breeding income qualifies.

## The dual-use trap that disqualifies most claims

Most dog deductions get denied for one reason: the dog lives a double life. It guards the shop by day and sleeps on the couch at night. It's called a service animal but has no documented task training. It's a "farm dog" that mostly rides in the truck.

The IRS looks for consistency between how you describe the dog and how it actually lives. If you can't separate personal use from business or medical use, the deduction usually doesn't survive. A second set of eyes before you file, not after, makes this easy to get right.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct my dog's food and vet bills if he just keeps mice out of my shop?**

General pest control by a dog that otherwise lives as a household pet usually doesn't meet the ordinary-and-necessary standard in [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). If the dog has a documented, primary working role on your business property, with consistent evidence of that use, the costs have a much stronger case.

**Does a therapy dog qualify for the same deduction as a service dog?**

Not automatically. [Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) is built around dogs trained to perform specific tasks tied to a diagnosed disability. Emotional support alone, without task-specific training documented by a physician, typically doesn't meet that standard.

**I run cattle and use a livestock guardian dog. What records should I keep?**

Keep receipts for food, veterinary care, and training, along with notes or photos showing the dog's working role and where it's primarily kept. That documentation is what separates a working farm dog from a family pet if the IRS ever asks questions.

**Can a breeding operation deduct dog-related expenses like a regular business?**

Yes, if the activity is run as an actual trade or business with a bank account, sales records, and consistent profit motive, rather than as a hobby. The hobby-versus-business distinction is one worth reviewing with your tax preparer before you claim significant deductions.
