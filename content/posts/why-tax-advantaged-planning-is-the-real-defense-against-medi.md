*By TruCount CPA  PC, Accounting, tax and advisory firm in South Dakota, serving clients in the Brookings area and throughout the Midwest*

A knee replacement, a cancer diagnosis, a child's emergency surgery. Nobody plans for these, yet most families face some version of it eventually. A savings cushion is a reasonable start, but it's not a plan. The families and business owners who come through a medical crisis with their finances intact usually set up their tax strategy before the bill arrived, not after.

The Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) has tracked for years how many households can't absorb a $400 surprise expense without borrowing or selling something. Medical bills routinely run into the thousands. What closes the gap is money set aside in the right account, plus a tax return built to capture every dollar the IRS already allows you to deduct.

## An emergency fund buys time, tax strategy buys relief

Cash reserves matter. Three to six months of expenses in the bank keeps a medical event from becoming a cash-flow crisis overnight. But an emergency fund is passive money, taxed the same whether it covers a car repair or a hospital stay.

A Health Savings Account works differently. Contributions go in pre-tax, the balance grows tax-free, and qualified medical withdrawals come out tax-free too. For 2024, the IRS allows HSA contributions up to $4,150 for self-only coverage and $8,300 for family coverage, with an extra $1,000 catch-up if you're 55 or older. A farm family on a high-deductible health plan can build a tax-advantaged reserve for medical costs, separate from the savings meant for equipment or land payments.

Unused HSA funds roll over year after year, with no use-it-or-lose-it deadline like a Flexible Spending Account. An FSA still works for predictable expenses like orthodontia or contacts, so it's worth pairing the two.

## What counts toward your medical expense deduction

Even without an HSA, you can deduct unreimbursed medical expenses that exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, but only if you itemize. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lays out what qualifies, and the list is broader than most people expect: mileage to appointments, certain home modifications for a disability, long-term care premiums, even some travel costs for treatment.

Timing is where planning matters. If your expenses will land near the 7.5% threshold anyway, bunching elective procedures, dental work, or vision correction into one calendar year can unlock a deduction that would otherwise disappear. Spread across two years, you might clear the threshold in neither. Have this conversation with your accountant in October, not April, because by filing time the decision is already gone.

## Building the plan before the bill shows up

The strongest position is running both strategies together: steady HSA contributions through the year, a general reserve for what the HSA doesn't cover, and an annual look at whether bunching elective medical expenses into one tax year makes sense.

For a farm operation, this gets more layered. A bad weather year can push income down and change how the 7.5% threshold behaves, and with multi-entity structures, medical expenses paid personally don't automatically flow the same way. Details like these get missed when tax prep happens once a year in a rush, which is what year-round [tax planning and preparation](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/business-tax) is built to catch.

Professional service owners face a related problem. A single-owner practice with no HSA and thin cash reserves is one surgery away from a cash-flow disruption, on top of lost billable hours. Setting up the accounts now is the cheap insurance most owners skip.

## Common Questions

**How much should I keep in an emergency fund versus an HSA?**

A reasonable split is three to six months of living expenses in a liquid emergency fund, with your HSA funded up to the annual limit if your plan qualifies. Treat the HSA as untouchable except for medical costs, since it offers tax-free growth and withdrawals for that purpose.

**Do I need to itemize to get any tax benefit from medical expenses?**

Yes, for the medical expense deduction, since it only helps if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. HSA contributions are different and reduce your taxable income whether or not you itemize, which makes the HSA the more reliable benefit for most households.

**Can I use HSA funds for a family member's medical bills?**

Yes, for your spouse and any tax dependents, even if they're not covered under your high-deductible health plan. Keep your receipts, since the IRS can ask for documentation that the withdrawal paid a qualified expense listed in Publication 502.

**What happens if my medical expenses don't hit the 7.5% AGI threshold?**

You get no deduction for that year under current law, which is why timing matters. Bunching predictable expenses like dental work or new glasses into a year when you're already near the threshold is a decision your accountant can help you time before December 31.
